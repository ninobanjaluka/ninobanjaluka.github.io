I'd warned them for a month.

The aluminum ladder on the Elektro floor in the Centrum, where i have worked had started making a sound I didn't like, a kind of tired, metallic groan, the sound of material giving up a little more every time someone climbed it. I wasn't the type to write on the equipment, and i was just a few months there, but I still did it. In black marker, on the rungs, I wrote what I'd already said out loud more than once: *dangerous*. Nobody replaced it. Nobody took it out of rotation. It just kept sitting there, and we kept climbing it, because there wasn't anything safer, at all times, close enough to hand.

On February 11, 2022, it was my turn to climb it one more time, and it wasn't there for me. I was alone on the floor, so no one there to hold them steady for me

I don't have a cinematic memory of the fall itself. I remember climbing, and I remember the platform under my feet simply stop existing. Then I was on the floor, and something in my back had gone from *structure* to *wreckage* in about a second and a half. I knew right away something is terribly wrong, and the dull pain in my back was the only thing that felt real. Nothing else.

## What I broke

I'd compression-fractured two vertebrae, L1 and L3, along with my pelvis, and split my scalp open. Two days later I was in surgery: a transpedicular stabilization, which is a clinical way of saying they opened up my back, straightened what they could, and bolted the rest together, two titanium rods and ten screws running from my mid-back down into my lower spine. I have a 21-centimeter scar to show for it, running down skin that used to just be skin.

What I remember most vividly isn't the pain, oddly. It's how strange the ordinary parts of the situation felt. Right after the fall, I asked, still half in shock, whether someone could call in to cover the floor for me, like the store, like the customers, still mattered more than the fact that I couldn't move normally. Colleagues who didn't know any better helped me into a chair, when the actual protocol was to not move me at all. I called my brother to come pick me up like I'd just missed a bus. None of it felt real. Then the ambulance arrived, and with it, the end of a life I'd taken completely for granted an hour earlier.

## What stayed

The medical assessment, done years later, put a number on it: a 25% permanent reduction in life capacity. That's the clinical shorthand for a longer list, a vertebra that healed at half its original height, a permanent curve in my spine where there used to be a straight line, numbness and cramping down my left leg, pain that shows up with weather changes, and hard limits on how long I can stand, walk, or carry weight. The kind of job I'd been doing, on my feet all day, climbing, lifting, reaching, isn't something I can do the same way again, maybe not at all.

There was also a stretch of pain I can actually chart, because someone made me quantify it for the record: sharp and constant for two weeks, moderate for eight, mild for another ten, the better part of five months where my body was the only thing on the agenda, whether I wanted that or not. And underneath the physical part, a quieter fear that took longer to fade: the sense that my body was no longer something I could trust to just work.

## Where things stand

There's a legal case still open around how this happened and how it was handled afterward, a process I'm not going to lay out in detail here, partly because it's ongoing and partly because I'll write about it when it's all over. What I'll say is that a ladder that had been flagged as unsafe for a month stayed in service anyway, and I was the one standing on it when it stopped holding.

I don't have a tidy ending for this yet. The rods and screws are permanent; so is the reduced version of what my back can do. What I have instead is a strange kind of before-and-after clarity, about how quickly "someone should really fix that" turns into an emergency room, and about how much of what we call normal life is just structural integrity we don't think about until it fails.

This is not over yet, and you can expect more to come. Way more than you might think.
