*June 4, 2026*

The Adriatic is not simply a sea. It is a consequence — the residue of collisions that began 250 million years ago and haven't entirely stopped. Its floor is ancient seabed. Its coastline is mountain range. Its islands are drowned peaks. Understanding what the Adriatic *is* requires going back much further than any human civilisation — back to when this part of the world was an open ocean, and Africa had not yet struck Europe.

##  Origins: The Tethys Ocean and the Birth of a Basin

### The Tethys

In the Triassic, roughly 250–200 million years ago, the landmass now called Europe was a subtropical shoreline on the edge of the **Tethys Ocean** — a vast, warm, shallow sea that separated Laurasia to the north from Gondwana to the south. The carbonate sediments deposited on the Tethyan shelf over hundreds of millions of years are the very limestone that forms the Dinaric Alps and the eastern Adriatic coast today. When you touch the white rock of a Dalmatian cliff, you are touching compressed sea floor from a world that no longer exists.

### African-European Collision

Beginning in the Cretaceous and accelerating through the Eocene (roughly 55–34 million years ago), the African plate began its long collision with Europe, closing the Tethys from the west and raising the Alps, Dinarides, and Apennines. The Adriatic microplate — a fragment of the old African craton — was caught between them. It did not subduct cleanly. Instead it buckled, fractured, and partly slid beneath the Dinaric chain to the east, producing the thrust faults responsible for the Dinaric mountain belt.

The Adriatic basin itself is a **foreland basin**: a structural depression formed in front of the Dinaric thrust belt as the crust flexed under the weight of the overthrusting mountains. The basin filled first with shallow marine sediments, then with deeper water deposits as subsidence continued.

### Messinian Salinity Crisis

About 5.96 million years ago, tectonic uplift caused the Mediterranean to become isolated from the Atlantic. Over roughly 600,000 years, the entire Mediterranean — including the proto-Adriatic — **evaporated almost completely**, leaving behind vast salt deposits kilometres thick. This event, the **Messinian Salinity Crisis**, is one of the most dramatic climatic and geological events of the Neogene. The reflooding began abruptly around 5.33 million years ago, when Atlantic waters breached the Gibraltar gateway — possibly in a catastrophic flood. The modern Mediterranean, and with it the modern Adriatic, was born in that event.

### Pleistocene: Ice Ages and a Dry Adriatic

The northern Adriatic basin is exceptionally shallow — rarely more than 35–40 metres deep, with an average of around 15 metres. During the glacial maxima of the Pleistocene, particularly the **Last Glacial Maximum** (~20,000 years ago), sea levels dropped by 120–130 metres globally. The entire northern Adriatic was dry land — a broad coastal plain extending from present-day Venice to beyond the mid-basin. The **Po, Adige, Tagliamento** and other rivers cut channels across this plain, some of which are still traceable on the seafloor as buried palaeovalleys.

Megafauna roamed the northern Adriatic plain: **woolly mammoths** (*Mammuthus primigenius*), **woolly rhinoceros** (*Coelodonta antiquitatis*), **cave hyenas**, and **steppe bison**. Dredged bones recovered by fishermen in the northern Adriatic confirm this. The fauna was continuous with the broader European steppe fauna, uninterrupted by sea.

Sea level rise began rapidly after ~18,000 BP, flooding the plain and creating the present coastline configuration by roughly 6,000–5,000 years ago. The islands of the eastern Adriatic are the submerged ridges of the **outer Dinaric chains** — their orientation (northwest-southeast, parallel to the mainland) reflects the structural grain of the fold-and-thrust belt beneath them.

## Geology and Karst

### The Dinaric Karst

The eastern Adriatic coast is underlain almost entirely by **Mesozoic carbonates** — limestones and dolomites deposited on the Tethyan platform from the Triassic through the Cretaceous. These rocks are exceptionally pure, with low clay and silicate content, which makes them highly susceptible to chemical dissolution by slightly acidic rainwater (carbonic acid formed when CO₂ dissolves in precipitation).

The result is **karst** — a landscape defined not by rivers and valleys but by internal drainage, sinkholes (*dolines*), cave systems, springs, and disappearing streams. The Dinaric karst is one of the best-developed and most extensive karst regions on Earth, and the English word "karst" itself derives from the **Kras plateau** at the northeastern corner of the Adriatic, in present-day Slovenia.

Key karst forms along the eastern Adriatic:

- **Dolines** — bowl-shaped depressions formed by surface dissolution or cave roof collapse. Some are hundreds of metres across.
- **Polja** — large flat-floored enclosed basins, often seasonally flooded, formed by structural and dissolution processes. The **Imotsko polje** behind the Dalmatian coast is one of the largest in the Dinarides, containing two lakes — **Crveno jezero** (Red Lake), whose underwater walls descend over 400 metres, making it one of the deepest lakes in Europe, and **Modro jezero** (Blue Lake).
- **Caves** — the Dinaric karst contains thousands of explored caves. **Vilinska jama** on Hvar, the **Vrelo cave** near Mrkonjić Grad (accessible only by boat inside the cave), and the vast systems of the Velebit range testify to the extent of underground drainage. **Lukina jama-Trojama** in the Velebit is over 1,431 metres deep — among the deepest cave systems in the world.
- **Submarine springs (*vrulje*)** — where karst aquifers drain directly into the sea, sometimes hundreds of metres offshore. The freshwater discharge from vrulje creates visible temperature and salinity haloclines in the sea around them. In places, freshwater springs emerge at depth below the Adriatic surface.

### The Western (Italian) Coast

The western Adriatic coast is geologically entirely different. The Italian coast is a low-energy depositional environment underlain by **Quaternary alluvial and marine sediments** — the Adriatic equivalent of a delta plain. The Po River alone delivers roughly 15 million tonnes of sediment to the Adriatic annually. The coast from Venice to Rimini is characterised by barrier beaches (*lidi*), lagoons, and mudflats. Venice itself stands on sediment that has been subsiding since antiquity, a process accelerated by groundwater extraction in the 20th century.

The sedimentary asymmetry between the two coasts — carbonate karst in the east, clastic alluvial in the west — fundamentally shapes the sea's biology, chemistry, and circulation.

## Oceanography

### Basin Structure and Circulation

The Adriatic is conventionally divided into three sub-basins:

1. **Northern Adriatic** — shallow (< 50 m), nutrient-rich, strongly influenced by Po river discharge and seasonal temperature extremes. Salinity is lower than the rest of the basin due to riverine input.
2. **Middle Adriatic** — characterised by the **Palagruža Sill** and the **Middle Adriatic Pit** (~260 m depth), the deepest point of the central basin.
3. **Southern Adriatic** — the deepest part, with the **South Adriatic Pit** reaching ~1,200 m. Strongly connected to the Ionian Sea through the Strait of Otranto.

### Thermohaline Circulation and Dense Water Formation

The Adriatic is not a passive body of water. It is a **source of dense water** for the eastern Mediterranean. During winter, cold dry winds — primarily the **Bora** (*bura*) — blow from the Dinaric plateau toward the sea, cooling and evaporating the surface water. When surface water becomes cold and sufficiently saline, it sinks. This **North Adriatic Dense Water (NAdDW)** flows southward along the bottom, exits through the Strait of Otranto, and contributes to the deep circulation of the Ionian and eastern Mediterranean.

The **Bora** deserves particular attention. It is a katabatic wind accelerated through gaps in the Dinaric range — particularly through mountain passes near Trieste, Senj, Split, and Dubrovnik. Wind speeds regularly exceed 100 km/h and can reach 200 km/h in exceptional events. The Bora not only drives dense water formation; it generates intense **upwelling** along the western Istrian coast, drawing cold, nutrient-rich water to the surface, and creates the characteristically rough, short-period sea state that gives the northern and middle Adriatic much of its character in autumn and winter.

### The Western Adriatic Current

A persistent **southward-flowing coastal current** runs along the Italian coast, driven by density gradients and wind forcing. This Western Adriatic Current (WAC) carries the plume of Po River water southward, keeping it against the Italian coast and maintaining the east-west gradient in water clarity, salinity, and biology. The eastern coast is generally clearer and more oligotrophic (nutrient-poor); the western coast more turbid and productive in the agricultural-runoff sense.

### Tides

The Adriatic is notably tidally active for a semi-enclosed Mediterranean basin. The northern Adriatic experiences tides up to 1.2 metres in the Venice lagoon — significant for the Mediterranean, which is nearly tideless elsewhere. The tidal oscillation is a result of **resonance**: the geometry of the Adriatic basin approximates a natural resonant frequency for the M2 tidal constituent. The phenomenon of *acqua alta* in Venice is the amplification of this tidal signal by storm surge and Sirocco winds pushing water northward against the basin's closed end.

## Palaeontology

<Image src={photo6} alt="Jadran" />



### Mesozoic Reefs and Rudists

The limestones of the Dinaric coast preserve extraordinary fossils from the Cretaceous. Among the most important are **rudists** — bizarre bivalve molluscs that evolved to become the primary reef-builders of the Cretaceous Tethys, filling the ecological role that corals dominate today. Rudist reefs formed the carbonate platform whose remains are now the cliffs and islands of the Dalmatian coast. Their extinction at the Cretaceous-Palaeogene boundary (~66 million years ago) ended one of the most unusual chapters in marine evolutionary history. Rudist fossils are abundant in road cuts and coastal exposures throughout the region.

### Eocene Nummulites

In exposures from the Eocene (roughly 50–34 million years ago), **nummulitic limestones** are common along the coast — dense accumulations of *Nummulites* foraminifera, single-celled organisms with coin-shaped calcite shells up to several centimetres in diameter. These foraminifera bloomed in the shallow, warm Tethyan sea that persisted in the region after the main Mesozoic platform carbonates were formed. Nummulitic limestones are macroscopically recognisable by their dense lensicular fossils and were used as building stone throughout antiquity — the Egyptian pyramids are largely constructed of nummulitic limestone.

### Pleistocene Megafauna of the Islands

Several Adriatic islands preserve remarkable Pleistocene vertebrate faunas. When sea levels dropped during glaciations, islands connected to the mainland and received continental fauna. In interglacials, rising sea levels isolated populations, producing **island dwarfism** — a well-documented evolutionary response to resource limitation. 

The island of **Lastovo (Lagosta)** and others preserve bones of **dwarf elephants** (*Palaeoloxodon* sp.) and **dwarf deer** (*Cervus* sp.) — insular populations that had shrunk dramatically over tens of thousands of years of isolation. Similar faunas occur on most large Mediterranean islands. The Adriatic examples are less studied than those of Cyprus or Malta but are genuinely significant.

### The Po Delta and Holocene Palaeontology

The northern Adriatic seafloor is a palaeontological archive. Sediment cores reveal continuous records of sedimentation, faunal change, and environmental oscillation through the Holocene. Dredged material — particularly from trawl fisheries — regularly recovers subfossil shells, bones of Pleistocene terrestrial mammals, and occasionally stone tools from the drowned coastal plains.

## V. Flora

### Posidonia oceanica — The Ancient Meadow

*Posidonia oceanica* is not an alga. It is a **flowering plant** — a monocot related to lilies — that colonised the sea in the Cretaceous and has formed the ecological foundation of the Mediterranean and Adriatic shallow subtidal ever since.

Posidonia meadows are among the most biologically rich habitats in the Adriatic. A single square metre supports hundreds of species — polychaete worms, crustaceans, molluscs, echinoderms, and fish — in a three-dimensional structure created by living and dead leaf litter. The meadow also stabilises sediment through its root-rhizome network, dampens wave energy, and produces substantial oxygen through photosynthesis.

What makes Posidonia extraordinary in temporal terms is its **growth rate and age**. Rhizomes extend by only 1–6 cm per year. Meadows measured off the Croatian and Montenegrin coasts have been dated by rhizome growth estimates and radiocarbon methods at **several thousand years old** — some potentially exceeding 100,000 years if the oldest clonal estimates are accepted, though these figures are contested. What is not contested is that these meadows predate every human structure along the coast.

On the beach, Posidonia produces **egagropili** (*morske kugle* in Croatian) — spherical balls of compressed dead leaf fibres, 5–15 cm in diameter, rolled by wave action and deposited on shorelines. They are sometimes mistaken for litter; they are ancient.

### Coastal Macroalgae

The Adriatic supports a diverse macroalgal flora. In the **photophilic zone** (well-lit shallow water), communities are dominated by **Cystoseira** species — large brown algae formerly present in extensive stands along rocky coasts. Cystoseira beds are a **bioindicator**: they are highly sensitive to eutrophication and physical disturbance. Their near-disappearance from the Italian coast and reduction along the Croatian coast is considered one of the clearer signals of coastal degradation over the past century.

**Caulerpa cylindracea** (formerly *C. racemosa* var. *cylindracea*) — a green alga native to Australia — is an established **invasive species** throughout the Mediterranean including the Adriatic, first recorded in the 1990s. It spreads rapidly by fragmentation, can colonise diverse substrates, and outcompetes native macroalgae and Posidonia margins. Its ecological impact in the Adriatic is ongoing and not fully quantified.

**Cymodocea nodosa** — a second seagrass species, smaller and faster-growing than Posidonia — forms meadows in shallower, sandier habitats and is more tolerant of disturbance.

### Terrestrial and Coastal Flora

The limestone karst of the eastern coast supports a **Mediterranean garrigue and maquis** (called *makija* or *šikara* locally) — dense, woody, drought-adapted shrubland. Dominant species include:

- **Holm oak** (*Quercus ilex*) — forming climax forest where not cleared
- **Aleppo pine** (*Pinus halepensis*) — widely planted but also naturally present on rocky substrates
- **Mastic tree** (*Pistacia lentiscus*)
- **Strawberry tree** (*Arbutus unedo*)
- **Phoenician juniper** (*Juniperus phoenicea*)
- **Kermes oak** (*Quercus coccifera*)
- **Rosemary** (*Salvia rosmarinus*), **lavender** (*Lavandula angustifolia*), **sage** (*Salvia officinalis*) — the last particularly associated with Dalmatian islands, where it grows wild

The upper karst, above the tree line, transitions to **dolomitic grasslands** and rocky plateaus with an endemic-rich flora. The **Velebit range** — the backbone of the Croatian coastal mountains — contains multiple endemic plant species, including the famous **Degenia velebitica** (Velebit catchfly, a tiny crucifer known only from a few locations on the Velebit limestone), found nowhere else on Earth.

## Fauna

### Fish

The Adriatic supports approximately **120 fish species** regularly, with additional occasional migrants from the warmer Ionian. The fauna is entirely Mediterranean in character, with no Atlantic or Black Sea endemics.

Key ecologically important species:

**Pelagic:**
- **European anchovy** (*Engraulis encrasicolus*) and **European sardine** (*Sardina pilchardus*) — the dominant small pelagics, critical to food web energy transfer and historically the foundation of Adriatic fisheries. Both are highly sensitive to plankton productivity and thermohaline conditions. Population dynamics are tightly coupled to the Bora-driven upwelling cycles and Po nutrient plumes.
- **Atlantic bluefin tuna** (*Thunnus thynnus*) — once abundant, now recovering slowly following decades of severe overfishing. The Adriatic is a significant spawning area for the eastern Atlantic-Mediterranean stock. Adult bluefin are apex predators; their presence or absence is a proxy for the health of the entire pelagic system.
- **Swordfish** (*Xiphias gladius*) and **albacore** (*Thunnus alalunga*) — present seasonally, migrating from the Ionian.

**Demersal:**
- **Common sole** (*Solea solea*) — dominant flatfish of the northern Adriatic mudflats
- **European hake** (*Merluccius merluccius*) — important predator in deeper middle and southern Adriatic; heavily trawled
- **Red mullet** (*Mullus barbatus*, *M. surmuletus*) — characteristic of sandy and muddy bottoms; benthic foragers
- **Conger eel** (*Conger conger*) — predator of rocky reefs
- **Dusky grouper** (*Epinephelus marginatus*) — a large, long-lived protogynous hermaphrodite (females become males with age); once widespread on rocky coasts, now rare outside marine reserves due to spearfishing

**Sharks and Rays:**
The Adriatic historically supported a diverse elasmobranch fauna. **Shortfin mako** (*Isurus oxyrinchus*), **blue shark** (*Prionace glauca*), **smooth hammerhead** (*Sphyrna zygaena*), and the **great white** (*Carcharodon carcharias*) were recorded in the 19th and early 20th century. All are now effectively absent from the Adriatic basin, eliminated by bycatch, targeted fishing, and finning over the course of the 20th century. **Common smooth-hound** (*Mustelus mustelus*) and **spiny dogfish** (*Squalus acanthias*) persist in reduced numbers. **Common skate** (*Dipturus batis*) and several smaller rays — **thornback ray** (*Raja clavata*), **common stingray** (*Dasyatis pastinaca*) — remain present.

### Invertebrates

The benthic invertebrate fauna of the Adriatic rocky coast is exceptionally diverse. The combination of clear water, complex limestone topography, and temperature seasonality produces a range of communities from the tidal fringe to the coralligenous zone.

**Coralligenous habitat** — biogenic reef formed by the accumulation of calcareous algae (*Lithophyllum*, *Mesophyllum*), bryozoans, and sponges in semi-dark, low-energy conditions — is one of the highest-diversity habitats in the Mediterranean. In the Adriatic, coralligenous communities occur from roughly 20 m down to 120 m, forming extensive frameworks on vertical walls and overhangs. Characteristic species include:

- **Red coral** (*Corallium rubrum*) — harvested since antiquity, now severely reduced shallower than 50 m; deep populations persist
- **Yellow gorgonian** (*Eunicella cavolini*) and **red gorgonian** (*Paramuricea clavata*) — sea fans forming forests on deeper vertical walls
- **Noble pen shell** (*Pinna nobilis*) — the largest bivalve in the Mediterranean, reaching over 1 metre; produces **byssus** (sea silk), an extraordinarily fine fibre used in pre-industrial textiles. Near-extinct across most of the Mediterranean following a mass mortality event caused by the haplosporidian parasite *Haplosporidium pinnae* beginning in 2016; Croatian populations have been among the most devastated.
- **European spiny lobster** (*Palinurus elephas*) — rocky reef predator, heavily depleted
- **Common octopus** (*Octopus vulgaris*), **European common cuttlefish** (*Sepia officinalis*), **European squid** (*Loligo vulgaris*) — cephalopods present throughout the basin

**Echinoderms** are diverse and ecologically important. **Purple sea urchin** (*Paracentrotus lividus*) is the dominant grazer on shallow rocky bottoms; where urchin populations are high and their predators (including fish and large invertebrates) have been removed by overfishing, **urchin barrens** replace macroalgal and Posidonia communities — an alternative stable state resistant to recovery.

**Loggerhead sea turtle** (*Caretta caretta*) nesting occurs on sandy beaches from Montenegro and Albania southward. The Adriatic is an important feeding ground throughout the northern Mediterranean population's range.

### Reptiles

Beyond *Caretta caretta*, the Adriatic coast and islands support significant herpetofauna, driven by the island biogeography of the archipelago.

- **Nose-horned viper** (*Vipera ammodytes*) — the most venomous snake in Europe, widespread on rocky karst; island populations show morphological differentiation
- **Four-lined snake** (*Elaphe quatuorlineata*) — large, non-venomous constrictor of scrubland and rocky terrain
- **Dalmatian wall lizard** (*Podarcis melisellensis*) — a species complex with extraordinary island-level divergence; different islands support genetically and morphologically distinct populations, making the Adriatic archipelago a living laboratory of **island evolution**. Studies of *Podarcis* on small Adriatic islands have documented colour morph variation, diet shifts, and intestinal microbiome changes over timescales of decades — among the fastest documented evolutionary changes in any vertebrate.
- **Balkan wall lizard** (*Podarcis tauricus*), **Italian wall lizard** (*Podarcis siculus*) and others — the wall lizard species complex of the eastern Adriatic remains incompletely resolved taxonomically; new island populations continue to be described.

### Birds

The Adriatic sits on the **Central European-Mediterranean flyway**, one of the major bird migration routes between Eurasian breeding grounds and African wintering areas. The sea itself and its coastal habitats channel millions of migrants each spring and autumn.

**Breeding species of note:**

- **Griffon vulture** (*Gyps fulvus*) — large colonies persist on the cliffs of **Cres** and **Krk** islands; the Croatian island populations are among the westernmost in the Balkans and have been the subject of long-term conservation management
- **Eleonora's falcon** (*Falco eleonorae*) — a specialist breeder on Adriatic sea stacks and cliffs; nests deliberately late in summer so that its nestlings are fed on autumn passerine migrants funnelled over the Adriatic on their way to Africa. The synchrony between falcon breeding and migration is a remarkable ecological adaptation.
- **European shag** (*Gulosus aristotelis*) — breeding on rocky coastlines
- **Yelkouan shearwater** (*Puffinus yelkouan*) — an endemic Mediterranean seabird; breeds in cliff burrows and crevices, spending most of its life at sea. Feeds on anchovy and sardine; population dynamics closely track small pelagic fish abundance.
- **Audouin's gull** (*Ichthyaetus audouinii*) — an endemic Mediterranean gull, breeding in small colonies on uninhabited islands; the world population is concentrated in the western Mediterranean but Adriatic colonies exist.

**Wetland and lagoon habitats** of the northern Adriatic — the **Po Delta**, the **Venice Lagoon**, the **Neretva delta** — support nationally and internationally important populations of breeding and wintering waterbirds including **purple heron** (*Ardea purpurea*), **little bittern** (*Ixobrychus minutus*), **white stork** (*Ciconia ciconia*), and numerous duck species.

### Mammals

**Mediterranean monk seal** (*Monachus monachus*) — one of the world's most endangered pinnipeds. Historical records place it throughout the Adriatic; by the late 20th century it had been extirpated from all accessible areas. Occasional sightings in remote Croatian cave coastline suggest a residual presence or recolonisation from the stronger Ionian population. Monk seals require undisturbed sea caves for pupping — a habitat now extremely rare along the Adriatic coast.

**Common bottlenose dolphin** (*Tursiops truncatus*) — the primary cetacean of the Adriatic. A resident population inhabits the Croatian archipelago and the Lošinj-Cres area in particular, where the **Blue World Institute** has conducted one of the longest continuous cetacean studies in the Mediterranean since 1987. The Lošinj bottlenose dolphin community shows strong site fidelity, individual recognition by natural marks, and culturally transmitted foraging behaviours. Population size is estimated at several hundred individuals across the northern and middle Adriatic, but is subject to bycatch mortality, boat disturbance, and prey depletion.

**Striped dolphin** (*Stenella coeruleoalba*) and **common dolphin** (*Delphinus delphis*) — present in the southern Adriatic and occasionally entering the middle basin, but not resident.

**Risso's dolphin** (*Grampus griseus*) — deep-water cephalopod specialist; occasional in the southern Adriatic pit.

## Invasive Species and Ecological Change

The Adriatic has absorbed successive waves of biological introductions. Some arrived through the **Suez Canal** (Lessepsian migration) or ship ballast water; others through aquaculture escapes or the aquarium trade.

**Notable established invaders:**

- *Caulerpa cylindracea* — as noted above, now widespread along rocky coasts
- **Pacific oyster** (*Magallana gigas*) — escaped from aquaculture, now establishing on natural substrates
- **Atlantic blue crab** (*Callinectes sapidus*) — introduced via ballast water; populations have exploded in the northern Adriatic since the 2010s, causing significant damage to coastal fisheries through predation on bivalves and disruption of benthic communities. Fishermen report catches of hundreds of kilograms per haul where the species was absent a decade ago.
- *Womersleyella setacea* — a red alga from the Indo-Pacific, now coating coralligenous substrates in parts of the basin

The blue crab expansion is perhaps the most dramatic ecological change in the Adriatic in recent decades — a trophic disruption with cascading effects on the benthic communities of the northern basin that were already stressed by decades of bottom trawling.

## What Remains

The Adriatic is not pristine. The northern basin has been commercially trawled since the 19th century; the eastern coast has experienced intensive coastal development since the 1960s. Large sharks are gone. Monk seals are functionally gone. Noble pen shells are dying. The upper thermocline warms at a measurable rate.

What remains is still exceptional. The middle and southern Croatian archipelago — the outer islands, the channels, the submarine walls — contains some of the most intact benthic habitat in the Mediterranean. The Bora still forms dense water. Posidonia meadows thousands of years old still grow on the seafloor of bays that were dry land before any human settlement in the region. Eleonora's falcons still time their breeding to the migration. Dolphins still learn from each other.

The Adriatic is a geological consequence, a biological community, and a set of ongoing processes — all interleaved with 250 million years of accumulated evidence. It is worth understanding on those terms.
