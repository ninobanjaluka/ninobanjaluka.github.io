There is no single reason why humans bury their dead. Burial in *Homo sapiens* is a **stacked behaviour** , practical corpse disposal, emotional attachment and grief, symbolic/religious meaning, social memory, and occasionally territorial signalling , layered on top of each other over 100,000+ years. Crucially, **burial itself is not the "human" part**; the cultural framing around it is. And the *Homo naledi* case shows exactly how hard it is to prove that framing from dirt alone.

---

## Two questions, often confused

**Functional:** why did the behaviour evolve and persist? (hygiene, grief, cohesion, territory)  
**Intentional:** what did the mourners think they were doing? (honouring, fearing, remembering, claiming)

Archaeology can sometimes answer the first. It almost never answers the second.

---

## Part I , Why *Homo sapiens* bury their dead

### 1. The practical layer: a corpse is a problem

The least romantic explanation, and probably the oldest. Humans have a strong **aversion to decay** , the smell, the flies, the scavengers, the pathogens. Semi-nomadic groups living in caves and rock shelters were literally sleeping next to their dead. Burying, covering, or moving a body is a hygienic and practical solution. Many researchers think the earliest burials were mostly this , with symbolism layered on later.

**Important calibration:** burial per se is *not* unique to humans. Chimpanzee mothers carry their dead infants for days; termites seal off dead colony members with sediment. Paul Pettitt (Durham):

> "One only has to think of chimpanzee mothers carrying the naturally dried corpses of their dead infants, or termites 'burying' dead conspecifics with sediment to understand that there is **nothing special about the simple process of burial per se**."

### 2. The emotional layer: grief and "continuing bonds"

The more interesting question is **when hominins began to feel attachment to the dead** , treating a corpse as a person who has left, rather than an object to be removed. Modern anthropology frames this as **continuing bonds**: the dead remain social members, and burial is the ritual that renegotiates that relationship. Burial becomes grief made material.

The problem: grief doesn't fossilise. Which is exactly why burial matters so much to researchers.

> "Use of language is the quintessential embodiment of such mental abstractions, but studying its history is difficult because language doesn't fossilize. **Burials do.**" , Paige Madison, *SAPIENS*

### 3. The symbolic layer: abstract thought and identity beyond death

Burial is the best-preserved evidence of **symbolic cognition** , thinking about things not physically present, remembering the past, imagining a future that includes your own death.

> "Burial rituals heightened the group's memory of the deceased person. These rituals may imply a belief that a person's identity extends beyond death." , Smithsonian Human Origins Program

Material markers of this:

- **Red ochre** at Qafzeh, Israel (~100,000 ya) , 71 pieces plus ochre-stained tools, found near the bones
- **Shell beads and pigments** , imported from far away, signalling identity, kinship or status
- **Grave goods** , antler, boar jaws, tortoise shell, flint
- **Consistent body positioning** , early *H. sapiens* almost always laid out in a **flexed fetal position**, head tucked to the chest

That last point is the strongest: the same posture repeated across scattered small populations over tens of thousands of years implies a **shared tradition**, not ad hoc disposal.

### 4. The social layer: burial as memory and group glue

Ritual does work for the living. A funeral fixes the deceased in collective memory, reaffirms who belongs to the group, transmits values and identity to the next generation, and creates a place , a grave , that anchors a landscape in story. This is why burials escalate from "hole in the ground" to monuments. Once you have agriculture, graves become claims of descent and inheritance. The logic was already there in the Palaeolithic, just smaller.

### 5. The competitive layer: graves as Paleolithic property deeds

The newest and most provocative hypothesis comes from **Omry Barzilai** (Haifa) and **Ella Been** (Tel Aviv), *L'Anthropologie* (2024). The puzzle: Neanderthals and *Homo sapiens* **started burying their dead at roughly the same time (~120,000–90,000 ya) in the same place , the Levant** , while competing for the same caves and resources. Their suggestion: burial was **territorial marking**.

> "A cave is an asset. Where species are meeting and interacting, they are defining boundaries." , Omry Barzilai (*National Geographic*)

Supporting pattern: when Neanderthals disappeared from the Levant ~50,000 ya, *H. sapiens* burials there largely **stop too** , as if the boundary-marking no longer had a purpose.

**Caveats:** small sample (5 Neanderthal + 2 *sapiens* sites). Graeme Barker (Cambridge): *"These things are never a silver bullet... You have to be careful with making trends out of two or three dots on a continental map."*

### 6. Deep time: a mosaic, not a switch

| Approx. date | What we see |
|---|---|
| ~430 kya | Sima de los Huesos, Spain , 28+ individuals in a pit (mortuary behaviour, not necessarily burial) |
| ~250–500 kya | *Homo naledi*, Rising Star Cave , **claimed** deliberate burial, heavily disputed |
| ~120–100 kya | Qafzeh & Skhul, Israel , earliest confident *H. sapiens* burials, ochre, grave goods |
| ~100 kya | Tinshemet Cave, Israel , multiple fetal-position burials with ochre staining (2025) |
| ~78 kya | Panga ya Saidi, Kenya , child in a shallow grave, fetal position (oldest in Africa) |
| ~60–70 kya | Neanderthal burials become consistent across Europe & W. Asia |
| ~40 kya | Mungo Lady, Australia , oldest known cremation |

Burial didn't appear once and spread. It emerged **piecemeal, repeatedly, and was sometimes lost**. Barker: *"Knowledge appears to have been acquired and lost, and acquired and lost."*

### 7. Two honest caveats

> **Burial is not universal.** Many cultures cremate, expose the dead (sky burial), or inter them in trees. If burial were a hardwired human instinct, that wouldn't happen. It's a **cultural solution to a universal problem**. So "why do humans bury their dead" is partly the wrong question. Better: *why did this particular solution become so dominant in so many places?*

> **We're reading minds from dirt.** The evidence tells us bodies were placed deliberately. It does **not** tell us whether the mourners believed in an afterlife, feared the ghost, or just wanted the smell gone. The Shanidar "flower burial" is the cautionary tale , a beautiful interpretation that collapsed when the pollen turned out to be rodent-transported.

**Best current synthesis:** burial began as practical corpse management, became entangled with grief and attachment, acquired symbolic meaning through pigment and goods, and was reinforced by social and territorial functions. Once all four layers were in place, burial stopped being about the body and became about meaning , which is why we still do it.

## Part II , The *Homo naledi* burial claim

One of the most contentious episodes in recent palaeoanthropology, and still moving.

### 8. The claim

In **June 2023**, Lee Berger's Rising Star team posted **three linked reviewed preprints** to eLife:

1. **"Evidence for deliberate burial of the dead by *Homo naledi*"** , Berger et al.
2. **"241,000 to 335,000 years old rock engravings made by *Homo naledi*"** , Berger et al.
3. **"Meaning-making behavior in a small-brained hominin"** , Fuentes et al.

The claim: *H. naledi* , cranial capacity **465–610 cc** (about a third of ours) , **dug pits, placed bodies in them, covered them with sediment, and carved geometric designs on cave walls nearby.** Announced alongside a **Netflix documentary** and a National Geographic media push. That packaging became as controversial as the science.

### 9. What the evidence actually was

> "We uncovered concentrations of abundant *Homo naledi* fossils including **articulated, matrix-supported skeletal regions consistent with rapid covering by sediment prior to the decomposition of soft tissue**." , eLife abstract

Three loci: **Hill Antechamber** ("Neo"), **Dinaledi Chamber** ("Leti"), and the re-analysed **Puzzle Box** area (min. 6 individuals). The core argument: bodies were **articulated** , covered *before* soft tissue decayed; sediment was **matrix-supported**, not gravity-sorted; **XRF** showed chemically distinct, neatly stratified layers away from the fossils but **jumbled** layers next to them , read as "someone dug a pit and refilled it"; **Particle size distribution (PSD)** more variable near skeletons , also read as dig-and-backfill. Slumping, water transport, decomposition-on-the-floor all ruled out.

Note the hedge , "most compatible," not "proven."

### 10. The rebuttals

#### a) Martinón-Torres et al., *Journal of Human Evolution*

**"No scientific evidence that *Homo naledi* buried their dead and produced rock art"** , María Martinón-Torres (CENIEH), Diego Garate, Andy Herries, Michael Petraglia.

- The **peer reviews were unanimous** in finding the evidence inadequate , yet preprints stayed public and were promoted to press
- **No grave cuts demonstrated.** A burial requires evidence of a deliberately excavated pit. None shown
- **Natural agents not excluded** , flooding water, sediment flow, post-depositional movement
- The **"engravings"** never shown to be human-made, let alone made by *naledi*

#### b) Foecke et al., *PaleoAnthropology*

**"No Geoarchaeological Evidence for Deliberate Burial by *Homo naledi*"** , Kimberly Foecke, Alain Queffelec, Robyn Pickering. This is the demolition job. They tried to **replicate** the XRF and PSD analyses and could not.

> "The theory and experimental design are **not supported by the realities of the sedimentary environment** and violate all prerequisites for conducting a study in the manner selected."

Specifically: reporting standards not met; likely errors in data acquisition; "deep structural issues with data analysis, visualization, and interpretation" plus misuse of statistics. Decisively: **even taking the data at face value**, properly analysed it shows nothing , *"all sample groups fully overlap in composition."* No detectable difference between "grave" sediment and surrounding cave floor.

Queffelec is notable , he **helped develop the XRF method** used in the 2021 Panga ya Saidi study. Not a hostile outsider to the technique.

> "I don't think anything has united the paleoanthropological community like this has, in saying, 'This is not how you should do it.' And we are a group of people that generally don't agree on anything." , Andy Herries (*Science*)

### 11. Timeline of the whole affair

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| Jun 2023 | Three preprints posted + Netflix doc + media blitz |

### 12. State of play

The official eLife assessment on the published version is a **split decision**:

> "One of the reviewers concludes that the findings convincingly demonstrate intentional burial practices, while another considers evidence for such an unambiguous conclusion to be incomplete given a lack of definitive knowledge around how the hominins got into the chamber."

Not retracted, not vindicated. Published with a documented disagreement.

### 13. The November 2026 twist , the *Cell* proteomics paper

Published **11 November 2026**: **Madupe et al., "Proteomic analysis of dental enamel from 20 *Homo naledi* individuals shows no male markers."** *Cell* 189(14):4415.

What they did: gentle **acid etching of tooth enamel** to recover ancient **peptides** , enamel is the hardest tissue in the body and shields proteins for hundreds of thousands of years. **23 teeth, at least 20 individuals** , the largest sample of any extinct hominin ever studied this way. Searched for **Amelogenin-Y**, the protein from a Y-chromosome gene. **Found none.**

The eye-catching interpretation: the Dinaledi Chamber may have been an **all-female deposit** , potentially the earliest known sex-segregated burial site made by a non-*sapiens* hominin.

> "The lack of male markers with the group is truly fascinating." , Marc Dickinson, University of York

**But the authors themselves flag the alternative:** a severe population bottleneck could have knocked out Y-chromosome amelogenin production in *naledi* generally, meaning some of those individuals were male and simply don't express the marker. This would also elegantly explain the decade-old puzzle , why the Dinaledi adults look so **morphologically uniform**.

> **This does *not* resolve the burial question.** It's about biological sex, not depositional process. And it's from the same research group, so it will get the same scrutiny.

## Part III , The v2 story: how the team answered their critics

The most interesting part of the saga, because the revision isn't cosmetic. It's a **strategic retreat and rebuild** , and it partially worked. One reviewer flipped from sceptic to convinced.

### 14. The big shift: test everything, in order

The original paper argued *for* burial. The critics' central complaint: it never seriously tested the alternatives. So v2 restructured the entire Results section around **testing hypotheses one at a time**:

1. Deposition on a **talus** (the old "they just fell down the chute" idea)
2. **Slow sedimentary burial** on a cave floor or in a natural depression
3. **Rapid burial by gravity-driven slumping**
4. Burial of **naturally mummified** remains
5. **Primary cultural burial**
6. **Secondary cultural burial** (new section)

That's a real concession. They went from "here's why it's burial" to "here's why it's not anything else, in order."

### 15. The vocabulary move , and it's clever

The single most important change. They **stopped saying "intentional burial"** and coined **"cultural burial."**

> "'Burial' as an unmodified term may refer to the passive covering of remains by sedimentary processes. Use of the term 'intentional burial' would raise the question of interpreting intent, which we do not presume... The relevant question in this case is whether the process of burial reflects **repeated behavior by a group**."

And:

> "There is a common assumption... that burial must by definition be symbolic. We do not take any position on that question."

**Why this matters:** it shrinks the claim dramatically. They're no longer defending "small-brained hominins had religion." They're defending "bodies were repeatedly placed in dug features." Far more defensible, far more testable , and it disarms the "you're overinterpreting" criticism by conceding it.

### 16. What they actually added

**Geochemistry, fully reworked** , major oxides analysed separately from trace and rare-earth elements; Harker variation plots; principal component analysis; fuller XRF method reporting; new claim: localised trace-element signatures exist *only* in sediment directly associated with the bones.

**Micromorphology.** Identified a laterally continuous layer of **laminated orange-red mudstone (LORM)** at what appears to be the maximum depth of fossil occurrence , severely broken under dense fossil accumulations, surviving only as clasts.

**Microtomography (micro-CT) of sediment.** Figure 10: six panels of CT sections from the Hill Antechamber showing sediment in direct contact with articulated bone , claimed as *"the first application of microtomography to sediment structure in association with a Pleistocene burial feature."*

**Puzzle Box reanalysis** , full individuation of fragments, refitting DH1–DH4 crania, spatial configuration of the DH7 partial skeleton.

**New spatial documentation:** photogrammetry and **LiDAR** floor maps, plus a cross-section showing relative elevation of excavation areas , partly to prove gravity-driven movement from the entry point is *geometrically impossible*.

**14 figures in the main text, 13 of them new.** Direct answer to "we can't see the bones."

### 17. How they handled each specific attack

**"You didn't do enough micromorphology"** , They audited the standard by checking what the critics' own accepted burials actually contain. Many had *less* micromorphology than Rising Star. *"Many researchers do not apply micromorphological techniques in their particular contexts."* Translation: you're holding us to a standard you didn't meet in your own papers.

**"Where's the pit?"** , They now present an explicit **minimal definition of burial** (pit dug by hominins, body placed, covered) plus criteria for recognising a pit , then argue that many *accepted* Pleistocene burials have no demonstrable pit either. Defensible, but the weakest flank. "Other people's evidence is also bad" ≠ "our evidence is good."

**"The cave is too hard to get into"** , Answer: *H. naledi*'s body plan makes it **far better suited** to squeezing through narrow passages than living humans.

**"That stone is a geofact, remove it"** , Reviewer 4 demanded it. They refused: *"Whether *Homo naledi* shaped the rock or not... it is unquestionably there. **Omitting this one object from the report would be simply dishonest.**""

**"The data were fitted to a narrative"** , They noted that other accepted burial papers frame results the same way. And an unusually honest admission: their *earlier* work had followed the critics' recommended "null hypothesis" approach, and that **actively hindered** them.

### 18. The review outcome , the headline

**Reviewer #2 flipped. Completely.**

Their v1 objections: no proper archaeothanatological analysis; missing/unclear illustrations of bone arrangements; no reconstruction of decomposition sequence. On v2:

> "The revised version of this study has **successfully addressed all the concerns** raised in my previous review and... I now think that the authors provide **sufficient evidence for the presence of 'repeated and patterned' deliberate burials**."

They specifically credit the authors for staying cautious , *"explicitly refraining from inferring 'symbolic' behavior or complex cognitive motivations."* **The vocabulary retreat is what converted them.**

**Reviewer #1 did not flip , and made the sharpest point.** Robbins et al. 2021 showed: before the **Dragon's Back block** collapsed (before ~241 ka), *H. naledi* could have entered via a **shaft in the roof of the Postbox Chamber**, walked a straight descending passage, and only had to climb a ~5 m sill. Dramatically easier than today's 20-cm squeeze , and **before the fossils were deposited**.

> - It weakens the "impossible access = must have been deliberate" argument
> - But it *also* means animals, water and other agents had easier access too , which is worse for the burial case, not better

Reviewer #1: *"perhaps other independent teams are allowed to do a full analysis since science is about replication."* That last line is the real complaint. Not about data , about **who gets to check the work.**

### 19. What v2 conspicuously does *not* fix

1. **The Foecke et al. replication failure is not rebutted, only out-manoeuvred.** Adding PCA and Harker plots is a *different analysis*, not a reproduction of the disputed one. Foecke's core finding , *"all sample groups fully overlap in composition"* , is never directly answered. **Biggest hole.**
2. **No independent team has ever excavated this material.** Every claim traces back to one group's trench.
3. **The engravings are a separate paper** and were not rehabilitated here.
4. **The "we can't see a pit because sediment is homogeneous" argument is unfalsifiable.** If the sediment is too homogeneous to show a pit, it's also too homogeneous to show a refill.

### 20. My read on v2

**v2 is genuinely a better paper, and the reviewers were right that it improved.** The hypothesis-by-hypothesis restructuring, the archaeothanatology, the new figures, the micro-CT work , real additions, not spin.

But notice the *shape* of the defence:

- "Our evidence is as good as the evidence at other accepted burials" , ✔ true
- "The standards you're applying weren't applied in your own papers" , ✔ true
- "You can't see a pit in sediment like this" , possibly true, but unfalsifiable
- "Access was easier back then" , true, but it undermines rather than supports

**Almost none of it is: "here is a new, independent, replicable test that settles the question."**

So: v2 successfully moves *naledi* from **"no evidence"** to **"evidence comparable in quality to other contested Pleistocene burials."** That's a real shift. It does not move it to "established."

And the vocabulary change , "cultural burial" , is both intellectually honest *and* the most effective rhetorical move in the document. They gave up the claim everyone was attacking (symbolism, intent, meaning) and kept the one they could defend (repeated patterned placement of bodies). That's how the Reviewer #2 flip happened.

## Scorecard

### What is established

- Rising Star contains an extraordinary assemblage of *H. naledi*, minimum 20+ individuals, ~241,000–335,000 years old
- The fossils are genuinely deep in a cave, in a chamber that is extremely hard to reach , real, weird, and demanding explanation
- The eLife reviews were negative; the XRF/PSD analyses have not been replicated; the Foecke et al. critique stands largely unanswered on the geochemistry
- The 2025 VoR is a materially better-argued paper, with one reviewer converted

### What is not established

- That *naledi* **excavated pits** (no grave cuts demonstrated)
- That the sediment signal is distinguishable from background at all
- That *naledi* made the engravings
- That any of this was **symbolic** rather than, say, repeated disposal of bodies into a natural fissure over generations
- That the Dinaledi deposit was all-female (the *Cell* paper's own alternative: Y-chromosome loss)

### What would actually settle it

1. **Fresh independent excavation** with micromorphology and photogrammetry from the start , not reanalysis of someone else's trench
2. **Blind replication** of the XRF on new, properly documented samples
3. **Direct dating of the engraving surfaces** , if they're 5,000 years old the whole symbolic package collapses
4. **Full raw data release**, which Foecke et al. say was never sufficient

---

> **The one-line version:** Humans bury their dead because a corpse is a practical problem, a grief made physical, a symbol of identity persisting past death, and occasionally a deed to the land , and *Homo naledi* shows that the hardest part isn't doing it, it's proving it from sediment 250,000 years later.
